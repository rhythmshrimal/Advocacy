For most people affected by a proceeding under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, the first tangible consequence is not an arrest. It is an order of **provisional attachment** - a bank account frozen, a property encumbered, a business disrupted - served with little warning and considerable effect.

Understanding what the statute permits, and by when, is the starting point for responding sensibly rather than reactively.

## What provisional attachment is

Under **Section 5 of the PMLA**, an authorised officer may provisionally attach property believed to be involved in money laundering, where there is reason to believe - recorded in writing - that the property is likely to be concealed, transferred, or dealt with in a manner that would frustrate confiscation proceedings.

Two features are worth noting immediately. The attachment is **provisional**, valid for a limited period unless confirmed. And it is founded on **reasons recorded in writing** - a requirement that is not a formality, since the sufficiency of those reasons is central to any challenge.

Importantly, attachment restrains dealing with the property. It does not, by itself, mean the property has been forfeited.

## The timeline the statute sets

As the Act currently stands:

- A provisional attachment order remains valid for **180 days**, unless confirmed by the Adjudicating Authority within that period.
- The officer is required to file a **complaint before the Adjudicating Authority within 30 days** of the attachment.
- The Adjudicating Authority issues a **notice of not less than 30 days**, calling upon the noticee to indicate the sources of income, earnings, or assets used to acquire the property, and the evidence relied upon.
- After hearing the parties, the Authority decides whether the property is involved in money laundering and whether to **confirm the attachment**.
- Where attachment is confirmed, it continues during investigation for a prescribed period, or during the pendency of proceedings relating to the offence before a court.

Missed timelines matter. Where the statutory scheme has not been complied with, the lapse is a substantive ground of challenge, not a technicality.

> The Supreme Court has upheld the attachment machinery while emphasising that its safeguards must be observed. Those safeguards are the defence.

## The adjudication hearing is the real opportunity

It is tempting to treat adjudication as a formality on the way to the Tribunal. That is a mistake. The adjudication stage is where the **documentary record is built** - the source of funds explained, the chain of acquisition established, and the connection between the property and the alleged proceeds of crime tested.

Two points make a practical difference here. First, a reply supported by contemporaneous documents - bank statements, returns, sale deeds, loan sanction letters - is far more effective than a reply that asserts legitimacy in general terms. Second, the burden of explaining sources is one the noticee is expressly invited to discharge, so silence is rarely a strategy.

## If the attachment is confirmed

Confirmation is not the end of the road. An appeal lies to the **Appellate Tribunal**, ordinarily within 45 days of receipt of the order, and from there to the **High Court**, within 60 days, on a question of law. Separately, where possession of attached property is taken, the statute and the rules provide safeguards around that process, and hardship in relation to residential property can be raised.

## The takeaway

The period immediately after a provisional attachment is short and consequential. Preserve every document that explains how the property was acquired, do not deal with the attached asset, and get advice before responding to the notice - because the record you create at adjudication is the record you will be arguing from for years afterwards.
