In most civil disputes worth fighting, the first real contest is not the trial - it is the interim application. Whether a property can be sold, a construction continued, an invocation of a guarantee stopped, or a termination given effect while the suit is pending often determines what is left to litigate about years later.

The remedy is a **temporary injunction under Order XXXIX of the Code of Civil Procedure**, and courts approach it through three well-settled tests.

## The three tests

**1. A prima facie case.** Not a proven case - the applicant must show a serious question to be tried and a case that, on the material placed on record, is arguable on its face. Courts do not decide the suit at this stage, but they will not restrain a party on a claim that discloses nothing.

**2. Irreparable injury.** The applicant must show that the harm feared cannot be adequately compensated in damages. This is where purely monetary claims often fail: if the grievance is that money is owed, the answer is usually a decree for money, not an injunction.

**3. Balance of convenience.** The court weighs the comparative inconvenience of granting the order against refusing it. An injunction that causes greater harm than it prevents will not be granted.

> All three must be satisfied. A strong case on the merits does not save an application where damages are an adequate remedy.

## What decides these applications in practice

Beyond the three tests, two things influence outcomes more than most litigants expect.

**Conduct.** Equitable relief calls for full and frank disclosure. Suppressing a material fact - an earlier order, a notice received, a settlement attempt - is a reliable way to lose an injunction, and to have one already obtained vacated.

**Timing.** A party who approaches the court promptly, before the situation changes on the ground, is in a materially stronger position than one who waits and then pleads urgency. Delay both weakens the claim of irreparable injury and invites the argument that the status quo now favours the other side.

## Ex parte orders and what follows

Where the matter is genuinely urgent, an injunction can be granted before the other side is heard. The Code treats this as an exception: the court must record its reasons for proceeding ex parte, and the applicant is required to serve the opposite party promptly with copies of the application and supporting material.

An ex parte order is inherently fragile. It is granted on one side's account of the facts and is liable to be modified or vacated once the other side appears. The correct expectation is that it buys protection until an informed hearing - not that it decides anything.

Courts also frequently balance the relief with conditions: an undertaking to compensate for loss if the suit fails, a deposit, security, or a direction that accounts be maintained. Where the injunction sought is negative, an order to maintain status quo is sometimes the practical compromise.

## Arbitration matters

Where the dispute is governed by an arbitration agreement, interim relief is available under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act - from the court before or during arbitral proceedings, and from the arbitral tribunal itself once it is constituted. Choosing the right forum at the outset avoids a preliminary objection that costs weeks.

## The takeaway

An interim application is not a preview of the trial; it is a distinct case with its own requirements. The applications that succeed are narrow, urgent, candid, and specific about the harm they seek to prevent. If something is about to happen that would make your suit pointless, that is the moment to move.
