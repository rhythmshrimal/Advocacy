Not every FIR should go to trial. Where a complaint does not disclose an offence, or where the criminal process is being used to settle what is really a private dispute, the law provides a remedy: the **inherent power of the High Court under Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure** to quash proceedings.

## What Section 482 protects

Section 482 preserves the High Court's inherent power to make orders necessary to:

- **give effect** to any order under the Code,
- **prevent abuse** of the process of any court, or
- **secure the ends of justice**.

It is a powerful remedy, and for that reason courts exercise it with care.

## When quashing is typically considered

Over the years, courts have identified categories of cases where quashing may be appropriate. Broadly, these include situations where:

1. The allegations, **even if taken at face value**, do not make out the offence alleged.
2. The proceedings are **manifestly malicious** or brought with an ulterior motive.
3. The dispute is **essentially civil** in nature and has been given a criminal colour.
4. There is a **legal bar** to the continuation of the proceedings.

> The test is not whether the accused is innocent, but whether allowing the proceedings to continue would be an abuse of process or a failure of justice.

## What courts will *not* do

A quashing petition is **not a mini-trial.** Courts will generally not weigh evidence or resolve disputed questions of fact at this stage — that is the function of the trial. An application that asks the court to do so is unlikely to succeed.

## The takeaway

A quashing petition is most effective when it is precise: it identifies, on the face of the record, why the proceedings cannot or should not continue. Brought in the right case and argued well, it can bring an unjustified prosecution to an early end.
