A departmental inquiry usually begins quietly - with a memorandum, a set of articles of charge, and a direction to submit a written statement of defence within a stated period. It is tempting to treat this as paperwork. It is not. **The reply to a charge memo is the foundation of the entire inquiry**, and much of what can be argued later depends on what was said, and preserved, at this stage.

## Read the memo as three separate documents

A charge memo is rarely a single accusation. It typically comprises:

1. The **articles of charge** - the specific misconduct alleged, usually tied to a conduct rule.
2. The **statement of imputations** - the factual narrative said to support each charge.
3. The **lists of documents and witnesses** relied upon by the department.

Each requires a different response. A charge may be vague or may not disclose misconduct under the rule invoked at all. An imputation may rest on documents that do not say what the department suggests. And a witness list may reveal that the case is thinner than the charge implies.

## Inspect the documents before you reply

You are entitled to inspect the documents relied on against you, and to seek copies of the relevant material. Replying before inspection - on memory and assumption - is the most common and most costly mistake in service matters.

Where documents that would help your defence are in the department's possession, ask for them in writing at this stage. A request made and refused is on the record; a request never made is difficult to complain about later.

> Every reply should be drafted on the assumption that it will be read years later by an appellate authority, a tribunal, and a High Court.

## What a considered reply does

A well-drafted written statement of defence:

- **Answers each article of charge specifically**, rather than offering a general denial.
- Distinguishes **fact from characterisation** - accepting what is documented and undeniable, while contesting the inference drawn from it.
- Identifies **procedural defects** at the threshold: an authority acting without jurisdiction, charges based on a matter already concluded, or unexplained delay in initiating proceedings.
- Avoids **admissions of convenience**. An admission made to appear cooperative will be relied upon at every subsequent stage.

## The inquiry that follows

If the charges are not admitted, an inquiry officer is appointed and the matter proceeds to evidence. Two safeguards matter here. You are entitled to a **defence assistant** to help present your case, and you are entitled to **cross-examine** the witnesses produced against you - a right that is frequently under-used.

At the conclusion, the inquiry report must be furnished to you before the disciplinary authority decides on penalty. The Supreme Court has held that supplying the report is not a formality: it exists so the employee can respond to the findings before consequences follow.

## Why procedure decides these cases

Service matters are often won on process rather than merits - because the rules exist precisely to ensure a fair hearing. A denied inspection, a witness never produced for cross-examination, a penalty imposed without considering the reply, an authority acting beyond its competence: each is a substantive ground of challenge before the Tribunal and the High Court, and each is far easier to establish if it was objected to when it happened.

## The takeaway

Do not reply in haste, and do not reply alone. A charge memo deserves the same care as a plaint - because in practical terms, that is what it is. Take advice before the deadline, not after the inquiry report arrives.
