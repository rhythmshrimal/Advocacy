Winning a civil suit is not the end of the matter. A decree declares what you are entitled to; it does not, by itself, deliver it. Turning a decree into money, possession, or performance is a separate proceeding governed largely by **Order XXI of the Code of Civil Procedure** - the longest and most technical part of the Code, and the stage at which many successful litigants lose momentum.

## Where to file

An execution petition is filed before the court that passed the decree, or before a court to which the decree has been transferred for execution - typically where the judgment debtor resides or holds property. Getting this right at the outset matters: filing in the wrong court produces months of delay before a single step is taken on the merits.

## The modes available

The Code offers several routes, and the choice is strategic rather than mechanical:

- **Attachment and sale of property** - immovable or movable property of the judgment debtor, sold and the proceeds applied to the decree.
- **Garnishee proceedings** - reaching money owed to the judgment debtor by a third party, including bank accounts.
- **Arrest and detention** of the judgment debtor in civil prison, available in limited circumstances and subject to safeguards; it is a means of pressure, not punishment for poverty.
- **Appointment of a receiver** to take charge of property or a business and realise income from it.
- **Delivery of possession**, where the decree is for possession of immovable property.

For a decree for specific performance or a mandatory direction, execution takes a different shape - and non-compliance may additionally invite contempt.

> The practical question in execution is rarely "what can the court order?" It is "what assets can actually be identified and reached?"

## Find the assets first

The most effective execution petitions are built on information. Property records, bank details, GST and income tax particulars, corporate filings, and details of receivables all help convert a decree into recovery. The Code also allows the court to require the judgment debtor to disclose assets, and to attach property before judgment in appropriate cases - a remedy worth considering during the suit itself, if there is a real risk of assets being dissipated.

## How execution gets delayed

Expect resistance, and expect it to be procedural:

- **Objections under Section 47** of the Code, raising questions relating to the execution, discharge, or satisfaction of the decree.
- **Third-party claims** to the attached property, which have to be adjudicated before sale.
- Applications for **instalments or time to pay.**
- Challenges to the **sale process** after auction.

Some of these are legitimate; many are dilatory. Anticipating them - clean pleadings, accurate property descriptions, proper valuation, strict compliance with the sale procedure - removes most of the openings.

## Do not sleep on limitation

Execution is not open-ended. Under the Limitation Act, the period for executing a decree is **twelve years** from the date the decree becomes enforceable, with a shorter period for a decree granting a mandatory injunction. Twelve years feels generous and disappears quickly, particularly where a party has been pursuing negotiation instead of execution. Where the decree arises from a commercial dispute, timelines and procedures under the Commercial Courts Act may also apply.

## The takeaway

Treat execution as the second half of the case, and plan for it before the first half ends. Identify assets early, file in the right court, and move promptly - because the value of a decree declines with every year it goes unenforced.
