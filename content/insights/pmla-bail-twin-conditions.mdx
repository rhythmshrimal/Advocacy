Bail under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act is among the most contested questions in economic-offence litigation. The reason is **Section 45**, which lays down what are commonly called the "twin conditions" for bail. Understanding what they require — and what they do not — is the starting point for any realistic conversation about bail in a PMLA matter.

## The twin conditions, in plain terms

Where the twin conditions apply, a court considering bail must be satisfied of two things:

1. That there are reasonable grounds to believe the accused is **not guilty** of the offence; and
2. That the accused is **not likely to commit any offence** while on bail.

These are conditions *in addition* to the ordinary considerations that govern bail. They raise the threshold — but they do not make bail impossible.

> The conditions are demanding, but they are not a bar. They call for a careful, fact-specific assessment — not a foregone conclusion.

## What this means in practice

The practical consequence is that **preparation matters more, not less.** An application that engages seriously with the material on record — rather than relying on general submissions — gives the court something to work with when applying the statutory test.

Several factors commonly feature in the analysis:

- The **stage of the proceedings** and the nature of the material relied upon.
- The **role attributed** to the accused in the alleged offence.
- **Personal circumstances**, including health, custody already undergone, and the likelihood of the trial concluding within a reasonable time.

## A note on prolonged incarceration

Courts have repeatedly recognised that the right to a speedy trial is a constitutional value, and that **prolonged incarceration before conviction** is a relevant consideration even where the twin conditions apply. Where a trial is unlikely to conclude in the foreseeable future, that delay can itself become a significant factor.

## The takeaway

Bail under the PMLA is hard, but it is decided on facts. The right approach is to build the application carefully around the record and the specific circumstances of the accused — and to do so early, with counsel who knows the statute.
