Few service disputes are felt as personally as being passed over. A colleague junior in the seniority list is promoted; you are not; and no reason is offered. The legal question that follows is narrower than it feels, but it is often answerable.

## The distinction that governs these cases

Indian service law draws a line that decides most promotion disputes at the outset:

- There is **no vested right to be promoted.**
- There *is* a **right to be considered** for promotion, fairly and in accordance with the rules.

A court will not ordinarily substitute its own assessment of merit for that of the Departmental Promotion Committee. What it will examine is whether you were considered at all, whether the correct rules and criteria were applied, and whether the process was tainted by irrelevant material or procedural unfairness.

## Grounds that are genuinely arguable

In practice, sustainable challenges tend to fall into recognisable categories:

- **Eligible candidates in the zone of consideration were left out**, or the zone itself was drawn contrary to the rules.
- The **criteria were altered midway**, or a benchmark was applied that the rules do not prescribe.
- The DPC relied on **adverse material never communicated** to the officer. Where a grading or adverse entry in a performance appraisal affects promotion prospects, the Supreme Court has held that it must be communicated so the officer has an opportunity to seek its upgradation - an uncommunicated entry cannot fairly be held against them.
- A **representation against an adverse entry was never decided**, and the entry was nonetheless acted upon.
- The **seniority list itself is wrong** - because the date of appointment, the quota-rota rule, or the treatment of a particular recruitment year has been misapplied.

> Seniority errors compound. An error made in one year's list can affect every promotion for a decade, which is why the list itself is often the right thing to challenge.

## The sealed cover procedure

Where disciplinary or criminal proceedings are pending against an officer at the time of consideration, the DPC's recommendation may be kept in a sealed cover rather than acted upon. If the proceedings later conclude in the officer's favour, the cover is to be opened and the recommendation given effect - with consequential benefits, including notional seniority from the due date, where warranted. Delay in opening a sealed cover after exoneration is itself a common and well-founded grievance.

## What relief looks like

It is worth being clear about outcomes. A successful challenge usually results in a **direction to reconsider** - a fresh DPC applying the correct criteria - rather than a direction to promote. Where the entitlement is established, courts and tribunals do grant retrospective or notional promotion, restored seniority, and consequential monetary benefits, though arrears are sometimes moderated.

## Act promptly

Delay is the single biggest obstacle in these matters. Once promotions are made and third parties have served in the promoted post for years, unsettling the position becomes progressively harder, and an otherwise good case can fail on laches alone. If you intend to challenge a supersession, the time to raise it is when it happens - through a representation first, and proceedings before the Tribunal or High Court if that fails.

## The takeaway

Ask a specific question rather than a general one: was I considered, on the correct rules, on material I had a chance to answer? Where the answer is no, the case is usually worth bringing. Where the answer is yes and you were simply assessed as less meritorious, litigation rarely helps.
